## 1. The one-paragraph version

Ben Patrick's system rests on one idea: **"Demand v Ability."** You get pain or injury when what life asks of a joint is more than the joint can handle. The fix is to raise the joint's ability **slowly, through its full range, without ever training through pain**, using "gradient scales." A gradient scale lays out every exercise from a level grandma can do up to NFL level. He starts **from the ground up**: sled and backward walking, then feet and shins, then knees, hips, spine, and the upper body last. He trains the "forbidden" positions that modern life and old textbooks told us to avoid: knees past the toes, deep squats, the back rounding, the hamstrings at full stretch. He calls this **"stretch-strength,"** meaning being strong in your flexible ranges. His 2026 summary of the whole system is: *"Timeless abilities on a gradient scale."*

## 3. Core principles (in his words)

1. **Demand v Ability.** "The entire theory of ATG comes down to Demand v Ability." "Human bodies adapt. Just slowly. And not infinitely." He always adds: "YOU HAVE NO GUARANTEES." In 2026 he applies the same idea to money and schooling.
2. **Never train through pain; regress instead.** "Pain is a communication, and I want you to work in the zone that does NOT hurt." Muscle burn is fine, joint pain is not. "It would be better to do more reps at the level that doesn't hurt, than to push into ANY pain." His series title "Every Regression Counts" makes the point that there are "infinite tiny levels" between easy and hard, and all of them are valid. (Commonly attributed slogans such as "regression is progression" were not found word-for-word.)
3. **Reverse out of what's overdone.** His 2022 founding principle is "get stronger in reverse to the pain." For knees that means backward walking and sled. For shoulders: "the reverse of throwing." For basketball: "forward and high are overdone. Backward and low are unbalanced. That's my knee system in a nutshell."
4. **Restore full range, at the level that doesn't hurt.** His second 2022 principle. Strength and flexibility should become "one and the same": "Anywhere I can go, I want to be strong there." In 2026 he added the **"radiating strength"** effect: you don't have to force range, because training hard in your comfortable range makes you a bit stronger beyond it.
5. **Ground up.** "If two trees have the same branches, but one has stronger roots, which do you think will last longest?" Lower legs "earn" the right to train the upper body. In 2026 he added **"rebuild from the ground"**: if moving through a range hurts, resist a load that stays on the ground (a sled that barely moves, a dumbbell too heavy to lift). The joint works without being moved into pain.
6. **Sled first.** The sled "isn't bearing down on you." It works as strength training, cardio and rehab at once, and it builds circulation to damaged joints. He has coached "over a hundred thousand" sled sessions and says nobody was ever hurt.
7. **Balanced exercise.** Forward and backward, fast and slow, high and low, strength and flexibility. "Stretch-strength: Youth is flexibility. Adulthood is strength. Aging is loss of both!"
8. **Little and often, enjoyed, for years.** "When in doubt: do less and ENJOY it." "Even one set is a win." He thinks in "weeks, months, YEARS." His biggest mistake was "treating it as short-term rehab." He estimates about 5 years of consistent work for an average person's knees.
9. **Don't see life through a straw.** This is Poliquin's line. In 2026 he credits history (Hackenschmidt, Ling, Simmons, Vermeil) and refuses to run down other methods: "Exercise is not a political election." "Our next generation needs unbiased history more than they need propaganda."
10. **Why "no knees over toes" happened.** He traces it to a 1970s university finding that knees past the toes means more knee pressure. That observation was correct; the conclusion, to avoid the position entirely, was wrong. Joints need "motion and compression" to stay healthy. He calls it "an understandable mistake."

## 4. The order he starts people in

**Knee pain (the Knee Ability Zero order):**
1. Backward walking, unloaded at first, then a sled or a resisted treadmill.
2. **Straight-leg calf raise.** The knee isn't loaded yet.
3. **Tibialis raise.** Back against a wall, lift the toes.
4. **Knee-over-toe (KOT) calf raise.** The knee is held forward without moving.
5. *Then* start moving the knee: **stepdown**, then **ATG split squat**, then **heel-elevated, counterbalanced full squat**.
6. Then the hips, the spine, and the upper body.
7. Start with 1–2 sets, often of about 25 reps to burnout at your pain-free level.

**Parents and grandparents ("simpler is better"):** 200 yd (or up to 5 min) of backward walking, done with a partner. Then 3×8 ATG split squats per side on a stairwell, holding the rail. Then 2 sets of a chin-up scale: hang, then hold at the top, then lower slowly, then full reps.

**Progression rule:** train at your pain-free level, often to burnout. When it's easy, move one notch along the scale: less help, lower, steeper, one leg, more weight, or the weight held closer to you. If it hurts, go back a notch. Use measurable standards and keep a log.

## 5. The exercise library

Doses are his own unless marked *(secondary)*.

### Lower legs
| Exercise | What and why | Typical dose |
|---|---|---|
| **Backward walk / backward sled** | Toes land first and the knee travels over the toes with no load pushing down on it. Builds downhill ability, the soleus and the feet. This is what got him off painkillers. Push through the big toes. | 100–200 yd warm-up; 5–10 min; 50–100 yd each way in the Pro programs |
| **Forward sled** | Works the shin and hip extension. Almost always paired with backward work. | 50 yd each way, heavy |
| **Tibialis raise** (wall, tib bar, kettlebell) | "Your tibialis is your first line of defense against both chronic and acute lower body injuries." Legs locked, 2 s pause at top and bottom. Standing farther from the wall makes it harder. Tib bar credited to Bob Gajda. | 25 reps (Zero step 1); 2×20; 1× burnout to maintain. Standard: tib bar 20% of bodyweight ×15 |
| **Calf raises**: straight-leg, bent-knee/soleus, KOT, slant, seated | The soleus protects the Achilles. Full range, 5 s hold at the top. Scale: two legs assisted, then two legs, then one leg assisted, then one leg, then one leg loaded. Standing calf + seated calf + tibialis is his "trifecta." | 2×15–25. Standards: single leg 25% BW ×10; seated 50% BW ×15 |
| **Petersen step-up** | Heel stays up, slow, at least one hand holding on. A "bridge from lower leg to upper leg." | 2×20 |

### Knees
| Exercise | What and why | Typical dose |
|---|---|---|
| **Stepdown / reverse (slant) step-up** (older name: "Patrick step") | Rebuilds the ability to go **downhill and downstairs**, which he says people lose most with age. Three ways to make it easier: less slope, a smaller step, more help. "2×25 is like a push-up for epic knee ability." | 2×20–25, 1–3× a week. Standard: 20+ reps on a slant from a 6" step |
| **Poliquin step-up** | Front heel raised on a wedge, 4–6" step. | 2×20 |
| **ATG split squat** (front-foot-elevated deep split squat, "Knees Over Toes Lunge") | "The single best exercise investment I ever put in my body." Back of the thigh covers the calf, rear knee stays off the floor, rear foot on its toes, torso natural. The real work is from 90° down. Easier: raise the front foot higher, hold a rail. Harder: lower, then flat ground, then dumbbells ("safer as you go lower"). Also stretches the rear hip flexor, which helps the lower back. | 3×5 with a 5 s stretch at the bottom (Zero); 3×8 (parents); up to 8×8 (Pro). Standard: 25% BW per hand ×8 |
| **Full squat, counterbalanced and heel-elevated** | Three dials: "How far down? How much weight? WHERE is the weight?" "The farther the weight is in front of you, the less knee pressure." Progression: plate at arm's length, then kettlebell, then barbell front, then back, then Hackenschmidt behind-the-back. Heel elevation is "useful but not vital." Also "low & slow": 10+ s down. | 3×5 with 5 s down; 10×1 with 10 s down (Pro). Standard: 100% BW ×6, controlled |
| **Slant squat ladder** | 20 pain-free reps on two legs, then single leg holding on with two hands, then one hand, then unassisted 10/side, then loaded. | — |
| **"Hooper Therapy"** | Bottom-half squat reps, because basketball "almost never bends below halfway." | 5×10, 30–60 s rest |

### Hamstrings
| Exercise | What and why | Typical dose |
|---|---|---|
| **Nordic curl / ham roller system** | "Makes a knee harder to rip apart." Progression: ham roller on 2 legs, then 2 legs up and 1 down, then 1 leg, then Nordic lowering only, then full Nordic. Alternates **Week A (machine curl) and Week B (Nordic)**. | 3×5 Nordic; 2×8 curls. Standards: 5 full Nordics; machine curl 85% BW ×8 |
| **Full-stretch RDL / staggered RDL** | Upper hamstring flexibility under load. His "#1 helper" for returning to running. | 3–4×8–15 |

### Lower back and spine
| Exercise | What and why | Typical dose |
|---|---|---|
| **Back extension, with deliberate rounding** | Three abilities: *resist* rounding, *round*, and *come out of* rounding. "First, flex the abs. Then round the body. Then slowly come OUT… Fully squeeze your butt." Kettlebell is his favorite load. Easier versions: a horizontal floor or wall hold, a handrail, a seated version. "One of the very few pieces of machinery I wouldn't want my family to live without." | 2×15–20; his own: 10–20 with 50–100 lb. Standard: 10 slow reps with 25 lb (women) / 45 lb (men) |
| **Weekly spine "minimum set"** | Done back to back: brace holding 100 lb, then flexion with 50 lb, then single-leg rotation, then side-on QL. | Once a week |
| **QL / 45° side bend** | Hands low, then hands behind head, then holding weight. Expect soreness when new. | 2×10; 1× to 20 |
| **Seated dumbbell deadlift / seated good morning** | Inner-thigh flexibility plus lower-back strength against rounding. | 2×10–20. Standard: 37.5% BW per hand ×20 |
| **Overhead from a full squat** (2026) | Learned from Poliquin, who got it from Klokov. Raising the arms in a full squat forces the muscles above the lower back to work. Scale: tummy time, then arms up in a heel-elevated squat, then broomstick, then loaded PVC, then 15-lb bar, then snatch. | — |
| *Jefferson curl, reverse hyper* | Associated with him online, but absent from his 2024–26 writing. Treat as older content. | — |

### Hips, core, mobility
| Exercise | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Couch stretch** | "My #1 most-used stretch over the last 10 years." Up to 45 s per side after each workout. A pulsing version is 2×20. |
| **Elephant walk** | Fold forward with hands supported, straighten one knee at a time. Up to 20/side. Standard: palms to the floor. |
| **Standing pancake pulse** | "The wider my feet go, the STRONGER my groin muscles have to be." Up to 20 reps. Standard: head to the floor. |
| **90/90 "piriformis push-up," Cossack squat, 4-way leg swings, pigeon** | Covers the outside, inside and side of the hip. |
| **L-sit** | Legs bent, then one straight, then both straight. Hip flexors and core. |
| **Hanging knee raise** | "My #1 ab exercise." Knees to triceps ×20, then straighten the legs. |
| **Hip-flexor strength** | Cable reverse squat (50% BW ×20). The **"Case Curl"**: on a lying ham-curl machine with the pad on the knees, lie back and drive the knee up. The **"Jamaican hip flexor"**: cable on the foot, standing on a step. Test every 14 days on a 5–10 rep scale. |

### Shoulders
- **Principle:** "The #1 way to hurt your shoulder? Throwing." Build strength in the reverse of throwing, and balance pressing with pulling.
- **Formula:** "1–3 sets of 6–8 reps with 4 seconds down, 1–3 times per week… my go-to formula for over a decade." Load goes from just the arm, to a soup can, to a light dumbbell. If moving through the range hurts, **resist from the ground**: try to lift a weight that's too heavy, and hold up to 4 s.
- **His 4 shoulder tests:**
  1. **Powell raise:** 10% BW ×8, 4 s down.
  2. **External rotation:** 10% BW ×8, 4 s down.
  3. **Row:** same weight and reps as your bench press.
  4. **Seated full-range press:** 25% BW per hand ×8.
- **Rings:** pull-ups, rows ("my overall favorite upper body exercise") and face pulls ("the most underrated exercise I've seen"), one set each to burnout. Also pullovers and full-range push-ups.

### Athletic: speed, jump, Olympic lifts
- **Return to running (7 steps):**
  1. Demand v Ability.
  2. Backward walking, then backward jogging, then backward running.
  3. Sled.
  4. Calf and tibialis work.
  5. **Non-motorized resisted treadmill** forward and backward. His main form of sprinting for 3 years. He calls electric treadmills dangerous for sprinting.
  6. RDL and ham roller.
  7. L-sit and split squat.
  - On hills: "fast up, down slow."
- **"Speed Truth" series (2026):** five parts covering shin angle (Su Bingtian, who squats 2.6× bodyweight), hamstrings (Week A/B; "I can't find a single elite sprinter who doesn't support sprinting with some form of hamstring strengthening"), hip flexors, and lower legs (Bolt used calf and tibialis machines).
- **Dunk plan (5 parts):**
  1. Deep knee strength.
  2. A palm-able foam ball so you can swing your arms.
  3. Adrenaline: a group, competition, sleep, and caffeine saved for dunk day.
  4. Lower legs.
  5. The backside: Nordics, RDLs, back extensions.
  - Also **Poliquin's "#1 exercise for vertical jump"**: a deep deadlift from a podium (hex bar). It rotates with front and back squats over 12 weeks at 3×8–10, then 4×6–8, then 5×4–6, then 6×2–4.
  - "A max jump is NOT a full squat." The jump itself still has to be practiced.
- **4-way athletic flow:** run 30 yd backward, left, right and forward; then 10 backward, 10 sideways and 10 forward jumps.
- **Snatch (2026):** Louie Simmons' kneeling-jump scale: assisted, then unassisted, then no arm swing, then front-loaded, then broomstick snatch, then barbell snatch. His "Snatch Progression for Grandpas" applies the same scale to older lifters.

### Equipment he relies on
- **Slant board:** "the most bang-for-your-buck lower body tool." About 25°, and it goes in his travel bag. He uses 4 foot positions on it.
- **Others:** sled or resisted treadmill, tib bar, back extension, ham roller, rings, and the **Swedish ladder** ("my #1 recommendation for schools," invented by Pehr Henrik Ling about 200 years ago; he doesn't sell one).
- **Budget versions:** a 2×4 under the heel, a $2 concrete block, $4 sandbags, a toilet-paper pack as padding for back-extension holds.

## 7. Body area by body area: his diagnosis and fix

| Area | His diagnosis | His fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Knees** | People lose downhill and downstairs ability, and strength "below the chair." Decades of "no knees over toes" left those ranges weak. "A deep squat is the single best overall knee injury preventer I've seen." | Backward first. Then the "$9 knee" big three: stepdown, split squat, counterbalanced full squat. |
| **Lower back** | "The #1 way to injure your lower back is bending over, usually to pick something up." Lost mobility in the ankle, hamstring or hip puts the back in a stressful position. "TIME is the #1 misunderstanding": gravity never goes away, so the basics are lifelong. The back is connected to everything else. | Back extension (all 3 abilities), QL, seated dumbbell deadlift, split squat, overhead from squat. |
| **Hips** | Chairs plus no sprinting gives "WEAK AND STIFF HIPS." Tight hip flexors pull on the lower spine. | Split squat plus back extension (his "Modern Pelvic Solution"), Cossack, pancake, couch, L-sit, Case Curl. |
| **Shoulders** | Overdone throwing and pressing. "Intent is the DRIVER. ATG is the BALANCE." | Reverse-of-throwing work, the 4 tests, rings, resisting from the ground. |
| **Feet, ankles, Achilles, shins** | Everything above the lower legs gets overdeveloped while bent-knee calf work is avoided, then come max running and jumping. Shoes are "NOT SHAPED LIKE FEET." | Forward and backward work plus full-range calf work. The 5-step shin routine. Foot-shaped shoes; barefoot where it's practical. |
| **Sprinting** | "Most adults will spend most of their lives never sprinting." The further you are from sprinting, the riskier it gets. | Backward running, resisted treadmill, hamstrings, hip flexors, lower legs. |
| **Jumping** | "I can't change your height… But I CAN change your knees." | Deep knee strength, lower legs, posterior chain, plus practicing the jump itself. |
| **Sitting / posture** | An "overly sitting, underly sprinting society." | Split squat plus back extension (the "1-2 punch"), sitting on the floor, rows. A 1:1 not-sitting ratio in his school policy. |
| **Aging** | Fear leads to less vigorous use, which leads to more loss. Healthy elderly people "still go up and down HILLS" and garden "with DEEP knee and back bending." | 1) "Joint-friendly cardio that STRENGTHENS" (sled, hill, resisted treadmill). 2) "DEEPER ability" (assisted, counterbalanced squats). Examples he cites: Mary Smith's 85–89 age-group 200 m world record, and Hackenschmidt jumping over chairs until 85. |
| **Youth** | Early specialization and "mileage abuse." High-school sport is "one of the top sources of knee surgery." | Late specialization, play, gymnastics, Swedish ladder, and 2 days of ATG a week. |
| **Neck** | No specific neck content anywhere. | — |

**Bodyweight vs free weights vs machines:** "both… can get the job done." Machines give precise measurement and are best for elite strength (ham curl, seated calf, back extension). Bodyweight and free weights are better for rebuilding from very low levels, because a machine starts with a load already on it. He prefers dumbbells for split squats, a hex bar over a barbell for safety, and kettlebells for back extensions.

## 10. What the evidence says

**Well supported:**
- **Knees past toes is not inherently dangerous.** Stopping the knee from travelling forward shifts load to the hips and lower back. Fry et al. 2003 found this; the direction holds up, although that study was tiny (7 men).
- **Joints and tendons adapt to progressive load,** and deep flexion is compatible with healthy knees.
- **Nordic curl programs cut hamstring injuries by up to ~51%** (van Dyk 2019 meta-analysis). One nuance: a 2020 meta-analysis found that exercise programs in general did this, and the Nordic added nothing extra on its own.
- **Backward walking reduces knee osteoarthritis pain** when added to normal rehab (small RCTs and a 13-trial meta-analysis).

**Overstated or untested:**
- **Tibialis:** he said "no study has ever been done" on strengthening it, which is false. No trial shows tib raises fix shin splints or knee pain. Reasonable and low-risk, but unproven.
- **Inner quad (VMO):** his claims about deep knee positions targeting it aren't backed by the studies cited.
- **Kneecap load:** the deep split squat puts more load on the kneecap joint. That's fine for knees that have adapted, but people with front-of-knee pain should scale it down, which his regressions do.
- **Personal and marketing claims** have no independent support: a 19→42" vertical, "most results with knees in strength history," "80% of surgeries unnecessary."
- **The ATG program as a package** has never been tested against other well-designed strength programs.

**What experts say:**
- **Dr. Chris Raynor (orthopaedic surgeon):** "largely grounded in sound biomechanical principles," and he praises the no-pain rule. But Ben is a coach without medical training who may oversimplify. Get a diagnosis for locking, giving way or swelling.
- **La Trobe physiotherapy researchers:** "The branding is new, the movement principles are not."
- **Matt Klein (PT professor):** he "is helping a lot of people," but the approach "can hurt a lot of people." Early relief may come from reduced fear.
- **Brad Schoenfeld:** no evidence that deliberately pushing the knees forward improves joint health.

**Verdict:** his central idea is right and well supported. The individual exercises are sensible and mostly long-established, and they're not his inventions. Much of the benefit probably comes from general factors: consistent, progressive, pain-free loading and less fear of movement. The marketing goes further than the evidence on a few specific claims.
