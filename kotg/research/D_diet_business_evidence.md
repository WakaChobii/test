## 1. Diet & lifestyle

## 3. Evidence and criticism

### 3.1 What is well supported
1. **"Knees should never pass the toes" is a myth for healthy people.**
   - Fry, Smith & Schilling (2003, JSCR 17(4):629-633): restricting forward knee travel in a bodyweight-loaded parallel back squat lowered knee torque (150 to 117 N·m) but raised hip torque sharply (28 to 303 N·m) and tilted the torso forward, implying more lumbar load. Seven trained men took part (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14636100/).
   - Caveats: the sample is tiny, and the hip-torque SD (±65) exceeds its mean (28). A later reanalysis reproduced the knee moments but called the roughly tenfold hip-torque change "rather unlikely" (https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/3248/3050).
   - The direction of the finding, that load shifts from knee to hip and back, is robust. The magnitude is not.
2. **Tissues adapt to progressive load; deep flexion is not inherently harmful.**
   - Two La Trobe researchers, Matt Savage and Adam Culvenor, wrote in The Conversation that knees past toes is normal, that loading keeps cartilage and bone healthy, and that deeper knee bends increase quadriceps work and may aid hypertrophy (https://dailymaverick.co.za/article/2026-09-01-can-knees-over-toes-exercises-help-bullet-proof-your-knees-two-experts-explain).
   - PT Erik Meira and George Fox professor Matt Klein say much the same in Outside (https://www.outsideonline.com/health/training-performance/knees-over-toes-guy-legit/).
   - Range-of-motion meta-analyses favour longer muscle lengths for hypertrophy (Pallarés 2021, cited by Sportsmith: https://www.sportsmith.co/articles/knees-over-toes/).
3. **The Nordic hamstring curl reduces hamstring injuries.**
   - van Dyk et al. 2019 (BJSM; 15 studies, 8,459 athletes): programmes that include the Nordic cut hamstring injury rate by **up to 51%** (RR 0.49-0.52) (https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2019/02/26/bjsports-2018-100045.long).
   - An earlier soccer-only meta-analysis gave a similar result (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27752982/).
   - Nuance: a 2020 meta-analysis of 17 studies found exercise interventions as a class gave RR 0.49, but **including the Nordic specifically did not add significant benefit** in subgroup analysis (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31680644/).
   - This is one of the strongest evidence pillars in ATG. Note that it is not his invention.
4. **Backward walking helps knee osteoarthritis symptoms.**
   - Alghadir et al. 2019 RCT (n=68; 6 weeks; 10 min, 3 times a week, on top of usual physio) found retro-walking beat controls on pain, function and quadriceps strength (https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12891-019-2537-9 ; protocol https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4830006).
   - A 2025-26 meta-analysis of 13 RCTs found backward walking **added to conventional rehab** reduced pain and disability (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40924430/).
   - Effects are modest, the trials are small, and the evidence is for OA, not "bulletproofing" healthy athletes.

### 3.2 What is plausible but thin, or overstated
- **Tibialis and tib bar claims.**
  - Ben wrote that "no study has ever been done on strengthening this muscle," and that force "not handled by the tibialis goes directly up to your knee" (Substack, Knee Ability Zero ch. 1, 2024-06-22).
  - The first claim is **overstated**: dorsiflexor strength is studied, for example in falls in older adults and in medial tibial stress syndrome, where tibialis anterior weakness appears in symptomatic legs (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8262020).
  - The second is a **mechanistic assertion without direct evidence**.
  - We found **no RCT showing tib raises reduce shin splints or knee pain** (MTSS review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18523568/).
  - Verdict: reasonable and low-risk, but unproven.
- **Vastus medialis (VMO) claims.** Sportsmith (coach Nathan Kiely) argues that the studies Ben cites do not show deep KOT positions preferentially recruit VM; VM EMG was about 30% lower at 140° than at 90° in Marchetti 2016 (https://www.sportsmith.co/articles/knees-over-toes/).
- **Patellofemoral load.**
  - Patellofemoral joint stress rises with depth in squats and is sensitive to lunge step length (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18632195/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11390050/).
  - So the KOT split squat is a *higher* patellofemoral load exercise. That is fine for adapted knees and a reason to scale for people with anterior knee pain, which Ben's regressions do (slant boards, heel elevation, assistance).
  - Savage and Culvenor advise starting with a smaller bend, less load and fewer reps, and accepting mild pain that settles by the next day.
- **No trials of the ATG program itself.** "The branding is new, the movement principles are not." The program "hasn't yet been well scientifically tested" against other well-designed strength programs (Savage and Culvenor, via Daily Maverick). Brad Schoenfeld is "not aware of any evidence" that *intentionally* pushing knees over toes improves joint health (https://menshealth.com.au/?p=46448).
- **Personal-result and marketing claims.**
  - A vertical jump of 19 to 42 inches, which BoxRox says to "take with a grain of salt" (https://www.boxrox.com/?p=163315).
  - An old site line that "Ben has achieved more results with knees than any person in strength history" (via BoxRox).
  - "80% of surgeries are unnecessary" (Saladino ep. summary) has no supporting source.
  - Ben concedes an old video title was a stretch (Ferriss).
- **Sport-specificity and lumbar claims.** Sportsmith argues the KOT split squat's "shin angle matches sprint acceleration" rationale confuses local and global reference frames, and that the exercise often features excess lumbar extension. These are coaching arguments, not data.

### 3.4 Credentials controversy
- Ben has **no degree or licence in physical therapy, medicine or exercise science** that we could find. His authority rests on his own rehab story and coaching experience.
  - The rehab story: at 18, a partial artificial kneecap, quad-tendon reattachment and meniscus transplant; years on painkillers; then a full-ride D1 scholarship at 23 that the NCAA eligibility clock blocked (JRE and Ferriss transcripts).
  - He mentions taking "college health courses" (Substack 2025-03-07).
  - He credits Charles Poliquin as his mentor.
- He sells ATG *certifications* to coaches. Critics see a self-referential credential, though it is common practice in the fitness industry.
- He declined to be interviewed for Outside's critical piece.
- He is **generally careful to point people to doctors and to train pain-free.** The "zero pain" rule is in his programs, and Raynor endorses it.

### 3.5 Balanced verdict
- **Right on the big idea.** The blanket "knees behind toes" rule was never evidence-based. Restricting knee travel shifts load to hips and spine (Fry 2003, direction robust). Progressive, full-range loading is how knees, tendons and cartilage adapt.
- **Popularized evidence-based staples.** Nordic curls have meta-analytic support for about half the hamstring-injury rate. Retro walking has small-RCT and meta-analytic support for knee OA pain. Deep squats are compatible with knee health. Slow eccentrics and regressions (slant boards, heel elevation) are sound rehab practice.
- **Over-reaches on specifics.**
  - Tibialis claims ("no study ever", force "goes directly to your knee").
  - VMO-specific claims.
  - Bold personal-result and marketing lines (jump inches, "most results in strength history", "80% of surgeries unnecessary").
  - The implicit suggestion that ATG beats other well-designed strength programs, which has never been tested.
- **Many effects are probably generic.** Exposure, reduced fear, consistency and progressive load likely explain much of the benefit, rather than unique ATG mechanics.
- **Risks** are mainly patellofemoral irritation from progressing too fast, and self-treating conditions that need diagnosis. His pain-free scaling mitigates these.
- **Overall:** the knees-over-toes principle has good support, the specific ATG exercises are reasonable but untested as a package, and the marketing claims are partly overstated.
